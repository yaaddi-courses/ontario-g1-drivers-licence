# Impaired and distracted driving, and demerit points

**Covers:** Impaired driving, Distracted driving, Demerit points

## Two ways to take your mind off the road {#overview}
Most serious crashes have a human cause, and two of the biggest are a driver whose body is not working as it should and a driver whose attention is somewhere else. Both are against the law in Ontario, and both can cost you your licence. This article explains each one and the points system that tracks the offences.

## Impaired driving {#impaired-driving}
Impaired driving means driving when alcohol or drugs have reduced your ability. The drug can be an illegal one, a prescription medicine that makes you drowsy or cannabis. You do not need to feel drunk: even small amounts slow your reactions, narrow your vision and weaken your judgment.

For novice drivers, the rule is stricter than for others. At G1 and G2 your blood alcohol must be zero. Penalties include an immediate licence suspension, fines and a criminal record for the serious cases. The only safe plans are not to drink or take drugs before driving, or to arrange a ride, taxi or designated driver beforehand.

## Distracted driving {#distracted-driving}
Distracted driving means driving while your attention is on something other than the road. The most common cause is a handheld phone, but eating, adjusting the music or the navigation, or looking at passengers counts too.

In Ontario it is illegal to hold and use a phone or other handheld device while driving, even at a red light. Display screens that are not part of navigation must also not show anything in your view. Looking away for just two seconds at 80 km/h means you travel more than 44 metres without watching the road. The simple fix is to set up your route and music before you move and put the phone out of reach.

## Demerit points {#demerit-points}
Demerit points are marks added to your driving record when you are convicted of certain traffic offences. A conviction on your record stays for two years, and the more serious the offence, the more points it carries. Speeding adds three to six points, and careless driving more.

As the points add up, the consequences grow: first a warning letter, then an interview, then a suspension. Novice drivers have lower limits than fully licensed drivers, so a few convictions can be enough to lose a G1 or G2. The Ministry of Transportation publishes the current table.

## A worked example {#example}
A G2 driver is convicted of two offences within the same year. The numbers below are an illustration, so use the official table for exact values.

| Offence | Points |
|---|---|
| Speeding 30 km/h over the limit | 4 |
| Using a handheld phone | 3 |
| Total on record | 7 |

Both convictions stay on the record for two years. With 7 points, this driver is already past the early warning level for a novice and may face a suspension. A second use of the phone in the same period adds more points and a bigger fine.

Compare that with the cost of the single choice that avoids both: staying under the limit and putting the phone in the glove box.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Thinking that one quick message or one drink is too small to matter. The risk of a crash rises sharply even for short distractions and low levels of alcohol. Another mistake is forgetting that points are not only about money: they can cost you your licence, which has a long-term effect on jobs and insurance.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Impaired driving covers alcohol, drugs and some medicines. G1 and G2 drivers must have zero blood alcohol.
- Distracted driving includes holding a phone, even at a red light. Prepare your route and music before you start.
- Demerit points stay on your record for two years, and enough of them lead to warnings and then a suspension.
- The simplest safe habits are the cheapest ones: plan a sober ride and put the phone away.
