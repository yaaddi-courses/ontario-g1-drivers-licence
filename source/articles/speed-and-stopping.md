# Speed, reaction and stopping distance

**Covers:** Reaction time, Stopping distance, Following distance

## Why speed hurts more than it seems {#overview}
When you decide to stop, the car does not stop at that moment. Between seeing a problem and the car standing still, it keeps travelling, first while your brain reacts and then while the brakes work. Both parts grow with speed, and that is why a small increase in speed can make a big difference to whether you stop in time.

In this article we will put numbers on the idea. They are rounded and use average conditions, so treat them as a guide, not a promise.

## Reaction time {#reaction-time}
Reaction time is how long you need to notice a hazard, decide what to do and move your foot to the brake. For an alert driver it is often around one to one and a half seconds. Tiredness, alcohol, a phone or a conversation all make it longer.

During that time you are still moving at full speed. At 50 km/h you cover about 14 metres every second, so one and a half seconds of reaction is over 20 metres before the brakes even start.

## Stopping distance {#stopping-distance}
The whole distance from the moment you see the hazard to the moment the car is stationary is the stopping distance. It has two parts: the distance travelled during your reaction time, plus the braking distance.

Braking distance grows with the square of the speed. Double the speed and the car needs about four times as much room to slow down. Wet roads, worn tires and snow make it longer again, sometimes by a lot.

## Following distance {#following-distance}
Following distance is the gap you leave behind the vehicle in front, and it buys you time to react. The usual guide is the two-second rule. Pick a fixed object, such as a sign. Watch the car ahead pass it, and count "one thousand and one, one thousand and two". You should not reach the object before you finish counting.

Two seconds is a minimum for good conditions. In rain, at night, on ice, or behind a large truck that blocks your view, leave more.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's assume an alert driver with a reaction time of 1.5 seconds on a dry road, where the car slows at about 7 metres per second each second.

| Speed | Metres per second | Reaction distance | Braking distance | Stopping distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 km/h | 13.9 | 20.8 m | 13.8 m | 34.6 m |
| 80 km/h | 22.2 | 33.3 m | 35.3 m | 68.6 m |

Read one row. At 50 km/h the car covers 13.9 m each second, so 1.5 seconds of reaction is 20.8 m, and braking adds 13.8 m, for 34.6 m in total.

Going from 50 to 80 km/h is a 60 percent increase in speed, but the stopping distance almost doubles, from about 35 to about 69 metres.

Now compare with the two-second gap. At 80 km/h, two seconds is about 44 metres, which is less than the 69 metres the table shows. The gap can still work because the car ahead also needs time and distance to stop. If it hits something solid, though, the gap is not enough. So in bad conditions, leave even more.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is judging distance by how close you feel. Distances look short at speed. Count the seconds instead.

A second slip is staying at the same gap when the weather changes, because wet roads can double the braking distance.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Reaction time is the delay before you start braking; the car keeps moving during it.
- Stopping distance is reaction distance plus braking distance, and it grows quickly with speed.
- Keep at least a two-second following distance, and more in bad weather or behind large vehicles.
- Slower speed and a bigger gap are the cheapest safety features you have.
