# Skids: when the tires stop gripping

**Covers:** Skid

## Grip is what steers the car {#overview}
Have you noticed that a car only turns, slows down or speeds up because its tires grip the road? Each tire touches the ground with an area about the size of your hand. When that grip is used up, the car keeps going where its momentum takes it, no matter what you do with the wheel or the pedals. That moment is a skid.

Knowing why skids happen is more useful than learning to recover from them, because the best skid is the one you never start.

## Skid {#skid}
In a skid, the tires lose their grip, so the car no longer goes where you steer or stops when you brake. The usual causes are driving too fast for the conditions, braking too hard, turning the wheel too sharply and driving on wet, icy or loose surfaces.

There are two common kinds. In a front-wheel skid, the front tires slide and the car goes straight on even though you turn the wheel. In a rear-wheel skid, the back of the car swings sideways. A hard brake can also lock the wheels, which makes any skid longer. Many cars have anti-lock brakes, which let you keep steering during hard braking, but they cannot create grip that the road does not have.

## How to recover {#recover}
If you are already sliding, stay calm and do things gently:
1. Take your foot off the accelerator.
2. Look where you want the car to go, not at the hazard. Your hands tend to follow your eyes.
3. Steer smoothly in that direction. If the back is sliding to the right, steer gently to the right.
4. If you have anti-lock brakes and need to brake, press firmly and hold. Without them, pump the brake lightly.
5. Do not slam the brakes or jerk the wheel. Doing so makes the skid worse.

When the tires grip again, the car can snap back quickly, so be ready to straighten the wheel.

## How to avoid skids {#avoid}
Most skids are the result of a choice made before the road went slippery. Reduce speed when the weather turns. Leave a longer gap, because your stopping distance can be several times longer on ice than on dry pavement. Brake before a curve, not in it. Check the tires, since poor tread and low pressure reduce grip. Be gentle with every control, and treat shiny patches on the road as ice.

## A worked example {#example}
You drive at 60 km/h on a wet road toward a curve that you could take at 50 km/h. Let's see what happens.

1. You brake hard inside the curve. The front tires slide wide.
2. You ease off the brake and the gas, because the car cannot both turn and brake at full strength.
3. You look at the lane ahead and steer gently toward it.
4. The front tires grip again, and the car follows your steering.

The safer approach is easy to see: slow to 50 km/h before the curve, then keep a steady speed through it. That uses far less of the tires' grip. Each tire has a limited amount of grip, and braking and turning draw from the same supply.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is looking at the thing you are afraid of hitting, such as a tree or a barrier. Your hands will steer toward it.

A second slip is thinking that all-wheel drive or anti-lock brakes make you immune. They help you accelerate or keep steering, but they do not give extra grip for turning or stopping.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A skid is a loss of tire grip, so steering and braking stop working as usual.
- Ease off the accelerator, look where you want to go and steer smoothly toward it.
- Avoid skids by slowing early, leaving a bigger gap and braking before curves.
- Braking and turning share the same limited grip.
